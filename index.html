United States · A living history
Before the beginning

No single story.
No simple timeline.

The history of the United States reaches back far beyond its founding. For thousands of years, Indigenous nations built communities, governed homelands, and sustained rich cultural traditions across the continent.

What follows is a starting point, not a complete account. The dates and turning points are connected to larger stories of sovereignty, migration, enslavement, resistance, invention, and change.

The timeline

Selected turning points · 10 chapters
Before 1492

Indigenous worlds

For millennia, diverse Indigenous peoples lived throughout North America. Nations including the Haudenosaunee, Cherokee, Diné, and countless others developed distinct systems of governance, trade, agriculture, and knowledge, adapted to their homelands.

There was no single Indigenous culture or political system: communities and nations were—and remain—distinct.

1492–1763

Colonization and encounter

European empires established colonies in the Americas, bringing warfare, land seizure, forced conversion, and epidemics that devastated Indigenous communities. Africans were forcibly transported and enslaved, while people from Europe and elsewhere migrated under varied conditions. Native nations negotiated, resisted, and adapted.

1763–1783

Revolution and independence

After conflict with Britain over taxation and political power, thirteen colonies declared independence in 1776. The Revolutionary War ended with British recognition of the United States in 1783. The new republic proclaimed liberty, while slavery persisted and Indigenous lands faced further encroachment.

1787–1820s

A constitution, a growing republic

The Constitution established a federal government in 1787, and the Bill of Rights followed in 1791. Political participation remained sharply limited. The country expanded westward through purchase and treaty, often under coercion, while the forced displacement of Native peoples and the institution of slavery continued.

1820s–1860

Expansion, removal, and abolition

Territorial growth intensified disputes over slavery and political power. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 led to the forced relocation of Native nations, including the Cherokee Trail of Tears. Enslaved people resisted in countless ways; abolitionists and free Black communities organized against slavery.

1861–1877

Civil War and Reconstruction

Eleven states seceded to preserve slavery, beginning the Civil War. Union victory ended legal slavery: the Thirteenth Amendment abolished it in 1865. Reconstruction amendments established citizenship and voting rights for Black men, but white supremacist violence and political retreat undermined those gains.

1877–1914

Industry, migration, and resistance

Rapid industrialization transformed work and cities. Millions immigrated, while Black Americans moved in the Great Migration's early years and Indigenous nations endured allotment and further land loss. Workers organized for safer conditions; segregation and racial violence shaped everyday life.

1914–1945

War, depression, and global power

The United States entered both world wars after years of debate about involvement. The Great Depression brought mass unemployment and the New Deal reshaped federal policy. During World War II, the government forcibly incarcerated Japanese Americans; the war ended after atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

1945–1980

Cold War and civil rights

As a global superpower, the United States competed with the Soviet Union and fought wars in Korea and Vietnam. At home, a broad civil rights movement challenged segregation and discrimination, helping bring landmark laws in 1964 and 1965. Other movements pressed for Native sovereignty, women's equality, and labor rights.

1980–today

A changing nation

Economic restructuring, immigration, and new technologies reshaped daily life. The end of the Cold War brought new conflicts and debates over America's role abroad. The September 11 attacks led to wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. Contemporary movements continue to contest inequality and define the country's democratic commitments.
